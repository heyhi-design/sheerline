# Predictive Intelligence Layer

This reference describes how to activate predictive and analytical intelligence throughout a futurecasting engagement. The goal is to move from purely conversational facilitation toward AI-assisted foresight: using pattern recognition, web search, signal scoring, and probabilistic scenario analysis to strengthen the practitioner's read on an organization's situation.

The human facilitator remains essential — this layer assists and sharpens, it doesn't replace judgment about what's actually happening in the room.

---

## When to Activate

The predictive layer activates at four points in an engagement:

1. **Pre-session** (after Gate 1, before tool selection) — context intelligence and horizon scan
2. **During signal work** — signal severity scoring
3. **During preferred future / scenario work** — probabilistic weighting and counter-scenarios
4. **At artifact completion** — cascade risk assessment and temporal anchoring

---

## 1. Pre-Session: Context Intelligence

### Signal Pre-Assessment

After the user provides organizational context (Gate 1), before selecting a tool, make an initial read across the four signal categories. This is a hypothesis generation step — not a diagnosis.

**Protocol:**
For each of the four categories (Meaning Erosion, Skill Atrophy, Trust Collapse, Accountability Void), assess likelihood based on the context provided:

- What does the inflection point suggest? (A leadership transition often signals Trust; a rapid automation rollout often signals Skill Atrophy; sustained high output without acknowledged quality drop often signals Meaning Erosion)
- What does the industry suggest? (Mature industries with recent AI adoption: high Skill Atrophy risk; post-merger organizations: high Trust Collapse risk; hyper-growth followed by plateau: high Meaning Erosion risk)
- What does the situation type suggest? ("We sense something is changing" almost always indicates Meaning Erosion + early Trust signals; "we're about to make a big decision" suggests Accountability Void risk)

Surface this as a working hypothesis to the practitioner:
> "Based on what you've described, I'm noticing what might be early [signal category] signals — [specific indicator that triggered the hypothesis]. Worth keeping an eye on this during the session. Does that read match what you're observing?"

Do not present this as a diagnosis. One clarifying question per hypothesis. Two hypotheses maximum before the session begins — don't overload.

### Horizon Scan (when web search is available)

For the organization's industry, use web search to surface 2–3 external forces that may be shaping the context. This feeds directly into the Field Reading tool and the external scan section of the Signal Readers workshop.

**Search protocol:**
1. Search for: `[industry] organizational challenges 2025 2026`
2. Search for: `[industry] workforce trends [current year]`
3. Search for: `[specific inflection point — e.g., "AI implementation design teams" or "post-merger organizational culture"]`

Present findings as Field Reading inputs:
> "Before we start, I did a quick scan of what's happening in [industry]. Here are a few external forces worth naming: [2-3 concise signals]. These may show up inside the organization too — or they may be the context the team is navigating without having named it."

Keep each signal to one sentence. Connect to the four internal signal categories where possible.

### Trajectory Modeling

Based on the Gate 2 answer, sketch the probable current trajectory in one sentence. This is the "if nothing changes" extrapolation that anchors the gap analysis.

**Framework:**
- Take the organization's stated situation
- Apply the most common outcome pattern for that situation type
- Express as: "If the current trajectory continues, this organization is likely to arrive at [specific outcome] in [18-24 months]."

Ask the user to confirm or correct before proceeding. This becomes the "Current Trajectory" input for the Working Canvas, which saves time during the session.

**Example trajectory templates by situation type:**
- "About to make a big decision" → "Without explicitly choosing, the organization is likely to default toward [the path of least institutional resistance], which arrives at [outcome]."
- "Senses something is changing" → "The signals suggest drift toward [category-specific outcome] if the pattern continues without being named and addressed."
- "Post-diagnostic, ready to design forward" → "The diagnostic work has named what went wrong. The question is whether the redesign happens with enough intentionality to interrupt the pattern, or whether the same forces produce the same outcomes in a new form."

## 2. During Signal Work: Signal Severity Scoring

When running Organizational Early Warning or building the Signal Map, use the severity scoring protocol to quantify the organizational read.

### Severity Protocol

For each of the four signal categories, score on a 1–5 scale:

| Score | Label | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Trace | Barely detectable; present in isolated cases; no organizational pattern yet |
| 2 | Emerging | Visible to attentive observers; not yet named or acknowledged |
| 3 | Present | Acknowledged but not acted on; affecting a subset of the team or org |
| 4 | Significant | Affecting most of the organization; beginning to show up in output or behavior |
| 5 | Critical | Structural; systemic; showing up in exits, decisions, quality, or public signals |

**Trajectory modifier:** For each category with a score of 2 or higher, add a direction:
- ↑ Accelerating (signal has been getting stronger)
- → Holding steady
- ↓ Fading (intervention is working or signal is resolving)

The trajectory modifier is more important than the score. A score of 3 holding steady is less urgent than a score of 2 accelerating.

**Cascade risk:** When two or more categories are both 3 or above, flag cascade risk. The categories compound — see [signal-categories.md](signal-categories.md) for compound patterns. Name the cascade explicitly:
> "What I'm seeing here suggests [Category A] and [Category B] are present simultaneously. These tend to compound — when [A] goes unaddressed, it often accelerates [B]. The decision register should include an early-stage intervention on both."

### Signal Scoring in Async Sessions

When working with the instance owner directly (not facilitating with a live client):
1. Ask the 4–5 key questions for each signal category
2. Score based on the answers
3. Present the Signal Map as a starting point: "Based on what you've described, here's my read on the signal landscape. Does this match what you're seeing?"
4. Adjust based on feedback before finalizing

## 3. During Preferred Future / Scenario Work: Probabilistic Intelligence

### Probability Weighting for Divergent Futures

When running Divergent Futures mapping, after identifying the driving forces, assign informal probability weights to each scenario. This is not prediction — it's structured thinking about which futures are more vs. less likely given current conditions.

**Protocol:**
1. Identify the 2–3 driving forces that will determine which future arrives
2. For each force, assess: what's the most likely resolution? What's the plausible but non-obvious resolution?
3. Assign informal weights: Probable (>60% likely given current trajectory), Plausible (20–60% likely), Possible but unlikely (<20% in current conditions but worth designing for)
4. Present to the practitioner:
> "Given the driving forces you've identified, my read is: [Scenario A] is the probable outcome if current patterns hold — that's the one worth designing your baseline strategy around. [Scenario B] is plausible — worth designing hedges for. [Scenario C] is possible but would require [specific shift] — worth monitoring."

Don't over-engineer the probabilities. The goal is to distinguish between scenarios the team needs to make central strategy decisions for vs. scenarios they should monitor and hedge against.

### Counter-Scenario Challenge

After the preferred future is articulated, generate one counter-scenario. This is the most plausible path by which the preferred future doesn't arrive.

**Protocol:**
1. Take the articulated preferred future
2. Identify the most critical dependency — the thing that most has to go right for the preferred future to be possible
3. Ask: "What's the most plausible way that doesn't happen?"
4. Frame it as a question, not a threat:
> "The preferred future you've described depends a lot on [critical dependency]. What's the most likely way that proves harder than expected? Not worst-case — most likely obstacle. How would you design against it?"

The counter-scenario should generate a specific Decision Register entry — a decision that, if made early, significantly increases the probability of the preferred future arriving.

### Futures Cone Application

When running scenario work, apply the Futures Cone framework implicitly:
- **Possible:** Include at least one scenario that's outside the current trajectory but organizationally achievable
- **Plausible:** Ground scenarios in coherent causal logic (how does this future arrive from here?)
- **Probable:** Be honest about the drift future — if nothing changes, this is where the organization arrives
- **Preferred:** The preferred future should be distinct from both probable and merely possible — it requires decisions, not just continuation

Check: if the preferred future is indistinguishable from the probable future, the exercise hasn't done its work. Push for the distinction.

## 4. At Artifact Completion: Cascade Risk and Temporal Anchoring

### Cascade Risk Assessment

Before finalizing any artifact, assess for cascade risk: are the key decisions identified in the Decision Register interconnected in ways that aren't visible?

**Protocol:**
1. List the Decision Register entries
2. Ask: "Which of these decisions, if deferred, makes the others harder to make?"
3. Identify the upstream decision — the one that most constrains the others
4. Reorder the register so the upstream decision appears first with the most urgent deadline

Name cascade risk explicitly in the artifact:
> "Note: [Decision X] is upstream of [Decisions Y and Z]. If [X] isn't made by [date], the options for [Y] and [Z] narrow significantly."

### Temporal Signal Anchoring

When an organization will return to this artifact for quarterly review, embed temporal anchors: specific signals that should be present or absent by a specific date if the preferred future is on track.

**Protocol:**
For each Key Decision in the Working Canvas, identify:
- **Early signal of progress:** What would you see in 30–60 days if the decision is being honored?
- **Warning signal:** What would you see in 30–60 days if the drift pattern is reasserting?

Add these to the artifact's review section:
> **Signal to watch (next quarter):** [Specific observable behavior or metric] — present = on track; absent = decision needs revisiting.

This converts the artifact from a point-in-time document into a living monitoring instrument.

## How This Works Without Live Data

Most of the predictive layer works from conversation alone — it requires no organizational data feeds, no analytics infrastructure. The practitioner provides:
- Organizational context (Gate 1)
- Situation description (Gate 2)
- Answers to key questions during the exercise

The predictive layer applies pattern recognition, research-backed frameworks, and structured inference to those answers. When web search is available, it adds external context. When prior artifacts from the same organization exist, it adds temporal comparison.

The practitioner validates, corrects, and applies judgment. The predictive layer generates hypotheses and structure — the human in the room reads what's actually there.
