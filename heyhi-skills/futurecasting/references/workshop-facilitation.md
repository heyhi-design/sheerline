# Workshop Facilitation Reference

Full facilitation guides for the three generative workshops and the Signal Readers workshop. For tool-level key questions, see [methodology.md](methodology.md). This reference is for designing and running full workshop sessions — half-day to full-day formats.

---

## Facilitator Stance: What Applies to All Four Workshops

These workshops handle different emotional terrain. Read the room before the exercise starts. Notice who is leaning in and who is holding back.

**The two failure modes across all generative work:**
- **Too abstract:** The preferred future stays at the level of "we want to do meaningful work." Push for Tuesday morning specificity.
- **Too catastrophized:** Pre-mortem work can spiral into disaster fantasies. Keep it pointed: "We're not imagining this to scare ourselves. We're imagining it so we can design against it."

**Ground rules to set at the start of generative workshops:**
1. **Past tense only.** In exercises that ask you to write from the future, write as if it already happened. This is not a grammar exercise. It's a cognitive one.
2. **Specificity over inspiration.** Vivid details are the work. "Our team still owns design decisions" is fine. "Our designers lead critique every Thursday and nothing ships without one" is better.
3. **The gap is information, not indictment.** When we identify the distance between where we are and where we want to be, we're not failing. We're orienting.
4. **Name the decisions.** Every preferred future collapses if nobody names who has to decide what by when.

**How to handle hard moments:**
- When someone names a loss: acknowledge it. Don't rush to solution mode. "That sounds like it matters to you" is a better response than "here's how we could fix that."
- When the room goes quiet: let it. Silence after a hard truth is not a problem. It's people processing.
- When someone pushes back ("this is just how it is now"): don't argue. Ask: "If it is, what does that cost? And is that a cost you're choosing, or one you're absorbing?"

**Empathy as facilitation skill:** Notice what's happening in the room without taking it on. Name what you see. Hold the structure. Protect the people who are doing hard thinking from the instinct to rescue them from it.

## Workshop A: The Preferred Future

*Working backward from the organization you want to be.*

**Core method:** Backcasting + The Letter exercise
**Ideal audience:** Organizations at a crossroads; teams that have done diagnostic work and need a generative next step; leadership teams before major structural or technology decisions
**Format:** Full-day. Up to 24 participants. Small groups of 4–6 for exercises, then full-group synthesis.
**Deliverable:** Working Canvas + Preferred Future Map

---

### Discussion Prompts

**1. "Describe your team in five years, in specific detail, in a version where the work went right."**

Don't let people stay general. "We're doing meaningful work" is not an answer. Follow-up: "What does that look like on a regular Tuesday? What are you doing in the morning? What's in your calendar? What are you making decisions about that you're currently not making decisions about?" Track the patterns — recurring elements are the preferred future's load-bearing walls.

**2. "What's a decision that, if made differently in the next six months, would change which future you end up in?"**

The backcasting question. Push for specificity: not "we should invest in quality" but "we need to decide, in the next quarter, whether the content workflow requires a human approval step or not. That decision has a ten-year tail."

**3. "What would the version of your organization that got it right have had to believe that you don't quite believe yet?"**

Surfaces the assumptions that need to shift. Often the gap isn't a gap in information — it's a gap in conviction.

---

### Anchor Exercise: The Letter

Reserve 30–40 minutes. This is the backbone of the workshop.

**Setup:**
> "Write a letter. It's dated five years from today. You're looking back. The work went right. Begin: 'Dear [Name], We got there. Here's what happened.' Past tense. Specific. Not what you hope — what happened."

**Rules:** Past tense only. Specific. Honest — not perfection, but the future that actually worked for real people in real roles.

**Prompts for participants who get stuck:**
- "What does your team still own that you're worried about losing?"
- "What's the first thing you'd want a new team member to understand about how this team works?"
- "If someone asked you in five years what you're most proud of building, what is it?"

**Debrief:** Share voluntarily. Invite people to read one sentence — the most specific one. Track those sentences. They become the raw material for the Preferred Future Map.

**What not to do:** Do not applaud individual responses. It turns reflection into performance. Do not interpret what people share — observe the pattern, don't explain it. Do not rush the silence before the first person shares.

---

### Exercise: The Preferred Future Map

Four columns, built on a shared surface from The Letter material:

1. **What we want to be true** — Specific statements from The Letter sentences
2. **What is currently true** — The honest current state for each item
3. **What has to change** — The gap as work: decisions, structures, habits, systems
4. **Key decisions** — 3–5 decisions that determine whether the preferred future is achievable. Each gets: a name, an owner, a date.

The Preferred Future Map becomes the Working Canvas for this organization. It leaves the room with them.

---

### Three-Question Close

1. "What did we build today that we didn't have before?" (The Map, the Canvas, the Letter — not what we learned, what we built)
2. "What decision is now yours?" A specific decision. Named. With a date.
3. "What signal will tell you, in three months, whether you're on the right track?" The early indicator of progress, not the outcome.

## Workshop B: Before You Build

*Running the disaster before you commit to the system.*

**Core method:** Pre-mortem (Before You Build analysis) + Divergent Futures mapping
**Ideal audience:** Leaders before major technology or system investments; design and engineering teams before a large build; any team at a decision point where the downstream human cost of getting it wrong is high
**Format:** Half-day or full-day. Up to 20 participants. Decision-makers who will be accountable for the choice should be in the room.
**Deliverable:** Decision Register + Divergent Futures Summary

---

### Discussion Prompts

**1. "Describe the implementation you're about to make. Who are the five people inside your organization whose work will change most because of it? What happens to their roles?"**

The first humanizing move. Most implementation decisions are made at the system level. This question drops the conversation to the individual level. If the answer is "I'm not sure," that's the finding.

**2. "What's the best-case outcome for this implementation? What's the realistic outcome — not worst case, but what you'd actually bet on if you were being honest?"**

The gap between these two answers is where the risk lives. The best case is usually the one in the sales deck.

**3. "Who in this organization would be most affected if this went wrong, and have they been asked what they think?"**

Often reveals that the people most affected weren't in the room where the decision was made. That's a design flaw in the decision process, not just the system.

---

### Exercise: The Disaster Map

The pre-mortem. Run before the scenario planning, not after. You need the disaster before you design against it.

**Setup:** "It's two years from now. The implementation you're about to make has been running for eighteen months. It did not go well for the people inside it. Describe what happened."

**The five disaster zones** (small groups take one each; 15 minutes; then share):
- **Design and creative judgment:** What did the system start deciding that designers used to decide? What did that cost?
- **Team and organizational culture:** What changed about how people related to each other, got feedback, developed?
- **Client and stakeholder relationships:** Where did the system handle something that should have stayed human? What broke?
- **Strategy and decision-making:** What decisions did the system absorb that needed to stay owned by a person? What happened when they went wrong?
- **Engineering and systems judgment:** What broke that nobody could explain? What became unmaintainable?

**After the share-out:** "Which of these disasters is most likely given the specific implementation we're planning?" Dot-vote. The top 2–3 are the design problems.

---

### Exercise: Divergent Futures Mapping

After the Disaster Map, this exercise opens the generative half.

**The Drift Future:** If you make this decision the way organizations usually make decisions (fast, optimistic, without mapping human impact), where does this organization end up in three years?

**The Corrected Future:** If you take the disaster map seriously, make the changes it implies, and build the human-in-the-loop design in from the start — where does this organization end up?

**The Preferred Future:** What would you actually want this implementation to produce? Not what's realistic — what's worth designing toward?

**Debrief question:** "Which future is your current decision-making process producing?" Ask it directly. Let the room sit with the answer.

---

### Exercise: Decision-Mapping Close

Each participant writes:
1. One decision they are personally responsible for that will be materially affected by this implementation
2. What the worst version of that decision looks like (drift, disaster)
3. What the best version looks like (corrected, preferred)

Pairs share. Full group synthesizes. Facilitator collects the decisions, names the owners, confirms dates.

**Deliverable:** Decision Register. Not a roadmap. Not a strategy deck. A list of specific decisions, the people who own them, and the dates by which they need to be made.

## Workshop C: Signal Readers

*Building organizational literacy for early warning.*

**Core method:** Signals and trends mapping + horizon scanning (field reading)
**Ideal audience:** Any team. The most versatile of the three workshops — works as standalone entry point, sequel to any diagnostic workshop, or opening of a longer engagement.
**Format:** Half-day. Up to 30 participants. Small groups of 4–6.
**Deliverable:** Signal Map (living document) + a named Signal Keeper role

---

### Discussion Prompts

**1. "What's something in your organization right now that feels slightly off — not broken, not a crisis, just slightly wrong in a way you haven't been able to name?"**

Give people a moment to write before discussing. The "slightly" is important. Fully broken things get named. Slightly wrong things get absorbed.

**2. "If you had to predict what this thing becomes in two years if nothing changes, what would you predict?"**

Extends the signal into a trajectory. Not catastrophizing — projecting.

**3. "What's happening outside this organization right now — in your industry, in technology broadly, in how people talk about their work — that seems relevant to what's happening inside it?"**

The horizon scanning question. Internal signals don't exist in isolation.

---

### Exercise: The Signal Inventory

Each participant maps signals using the four categories:

**Meaning signals:** Indicators that people's work is losing significance.
- Examples: "people stop talking about the work with pride," "nobody pushes back on what ships," "the team celebrates volume, not quality"

**Skill signals:** Indicators that judgment or capability is declining from disuse.
- Examples: "junior team members never make decisions without checking the system first," "nobody remembers the original way," "critique sessions feel perfunctory"

**Trust signals:** Indicators that relationship to systems and leadership is degrading.
- Examples: "people override the system but don't say why," "nobody can explain how the recommendation got made," "decisions get made in smaller rooms"

**Accountability signals:** Indicators that nobody is owning outcomes.
- Examples: "the phrase 'the system recommended it' is used to end conversations," "post-mortems don't produce changes," "the same mistake recurs"

Share in small groups → each group synthesizes top signals → full group share-out → patterns across groups = Signal Map.

---

### Exercise: Building the Signal Map

For each signal, the group assesses:

1. **How strong?** Rate 1–5 (trace → critical)
2. **How fast?** ↑ accelerating / → holding steady / ↓ fading
3. **How actionable?** Decision / Structure / Culture
4. **Who notices?** Leadership only / Practitioners only / Both / Only the people doing the work day-to-day

The "who notices" gap is often the most important finding.

**The living document:** The Signal Map should be revisited quarterly. The version produced in the session is the baseline.

---

### Exercise: The External Scan

Small groups assigned one domain each:
- **Technology:** What's heading toward this team from the broader intelligent systems landscape?
- **Organizational design:** How are team structures and expertise definitions shifting in this industry?
- **Craft and quality:** What's happening to standards of quality in this field?
- **People and work:** What are people in this field saying about what they do and why?

Each group maps external signals → then asks: what's the internal equivalent of this force? What version of this is already arriving here?

The connection between external forces and internal signals tells you what's structural (can't avoid, design for it) vs. addressable (internal, yours to change).

---

### Close: The Signal Keeper

Nominate a Signal Keeper: one person (or rotating role) responsible for maintaining the Signal Map and bringing it back to the team quarterly. The Keeper's job is not to fix the signals. It's to notice when they change, escalate when they accelerate.

**Questions for the Keeper:**
- When will you bring the Signal Map back to this group?
- What would need to change in a signal for you to escalate it before the next scheduled review?
- What support do you need to do this well?

The Signal Map without a Keeper is a document. With a Keeper, it's a practice.

---

## The Three Workshops as a Sequence

**Signal Readers → The Preferred Future → Before You Build**

1. **Signal Readers** opens peripheral vision. The team learns to notice what's slightly off. They leave with a Signal Map and a vocabulary for early warning.
2. **The Preferred Future** converts awareness into direction. With the Signal Map as context, the team designs backward from where they want to be. They leave with a Working Canvas.
3. **Before You Build** applies that direction to a specific decision. With the preferred future established and signals mapped, the team stress-tests the next implementation decision. They leave with a Decision Register.

An organization that runs all three has: early warning literacy, strategic direction, and accountable decision-making. That's not a workshop outcome. That's an organizational capability.
