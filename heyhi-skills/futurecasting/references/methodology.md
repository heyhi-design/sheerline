## The Facilitation Arc

A full Strategic Foresight engagement moves through four phases:

**Phase 1: Reading the Signals (morning)**
Surface what's actually happening, organized by the four signal categories. Create conditions where people say what they know, not what's safe to say. Technique: silent individual writing before group discussion.

*Note on routing — burnout vs. meaning erosion:* When a team says "people are burning out," clarify before proceeding. Burnout is a high-demand signal: the work is overwhelming, and the intervention is structural workload change. Meaning erosion is a low-connection signal: the work is manageable but empty, and the intervention is reconnecting to purpose or changing the nature of the work. The surface presentations overlap — disengagement, declining quality, flatness — but the causes and interventions don't. A team experiencing burnout needs diagnostic work and recovery, not a preferred future workshop. A team experiencing meaning erosion is ready to design forward. Use the diagnostic: if energy rises when people talk about doing *less*, route to diagnostic. If it rises when they talk about doing *different work*, continue with generative work.

**Phase 2: The Current Trajectory (late morning)**
Extrapolate from the signals: if nothing changes, where does this go? This often produces discomfort — that's the point. Technique: "Letter from the future" — someone writes what happened, from 18 months out, in the current trajectory.

**Phase 3: The Preferred Future and the Gap (early afternoon)**
Define what the organization actually wants to be true. Then name the gap honestly. Not identifying the gap — agreeing that it exists. Technique: The Preferred Future Map (four quadrants).

**Phase 4: The Decision Register (afternoon)**
Working backward from the preferred future: what decisions determine which future arrives? Name each one, assign an owner, set a date. Technique: Decision mapping — start from the preferred future, work backward.

## The Seven Tools: Key Questions and Facilitation Notes

### 1. The Preferred Future Method (Backcasting)

**When to use:** Any situation where the team has been stuck in problem-identification mode. When the room has named what's wrong but needs to design differently. When you need to shift energy from diagnosis to direction.

**The method:** Start from the future you actually want — specific enough that people would recognize it if it arrived. Then work backward to the decisions that would have had to be made to get there.

**Key questions:**
1. "If this went right — really right — what would your organization look like in three years?"
2. "What specifically would be different? Not 'we'd work better.' What would a Tuesday look like?"
3. "What decisions would have had to happen to get there?"
4. "Which of those decisions hasn't been made yet?"
5. "What's in the way of making it?"

**Facilitation note:** Push hard on specificity. Abstract answers ("we'd be more collaborative") need pressure. "What does that look like, specifically, in practice?" The preferred future is only useful if it connects to present decisions.

**Connection to HeyHi's work:** The Preferred Future method is what separates a Strategy Audit from a design system review. A team that has just received a Design Intelligence Audit knows what's broken in the present. The Preferred Future session converts that diagnosis into direction — what does this organization want to be designing in three years, and what decisions get it there? This is the tool that makes the Consult tier distinct from delivery work.

**Artifact:** Preferred Future Map → Working Canvas

### 2. Organizational Early Warning (Signals and Trends Mapping)

**When to use:** At the opening of any engagement. As an ongoing quarterly practice for retainer clients. When a team senses something is wrong but can't articulate what yet.

**The method:** Identify weak signals — early, often-overlooked indicators of emerging change — before they compound into crises. Weak signals are things that feel slightly off, slightly strange, or slightly uncomfortable. They're not yet trends. They're the precursors.

**Key questions:**
1. "What feels slightly off right now that you haven't said out loud yet?"
2. "Where are people doing things manually because they don't trust the system?"
3. "What's the last decision that felt strange or unclear after the fact?"
4. "Which conversations keep happening without resolution?"
5. "What would you not want a new hire to see in their first week?"

**Facilitation note:** Silence after these questions is productive. Don't rush to fill it. Give people a moment to write before discussing — what people write in private is often more honest than what they say in the room.

**Connection to HeyHi's work:** Signal reading is the organizational equivalent of the Design Intelligence Audit's drift assessment. The Audit reads drift between design intent and code reality. Signal Readers reads drift between organizational intent and organizational reality. Both are early warning practices. For clients on ongoing retainers, the Signal Map functions as a quarterly check-in instrument — it tracks whether the signals from the last session are accelerating, holding, or fading. This is how a Consult engagement becomes a Care engagement: the practitioner keeps the Signal Map alive between sessions.

**Artifact:** Signal Map

### 3. Divergent Futures Mapping (Scenario Planning)

**When to use:** Before major implementation decisions. In "Before You Build" workshops. When a leadership team is choosing between strategic paths. When the organization needs to build robustness rather than bet on one future.

**The method:** Develop 2–3 plausible future narratives based on different assumptions about how driving forces will play out. The goal isn't to predict which one will happen. It's to stress-test decisions across all of them — so that the team can build strategies that work across multiple scenarios.

**Key questions:**
1. "What are the two or three biggest forces affecting your organization right now — things you can't fully control?"
2. "If those forces resolved one way, where do you land in three years?"
3. "If they resolved the opposite way, where do you land?"
4. "Which scenario are you currently building toward — intentionally or by default?"
5. "What decisions now would make the best-case scenario more likely?"

**Three scenario arc:**
- **The Drift Future:** Where the current trajectory takes you if no significant decisions change. Unglamorous. Not catastrophic. Just quiet and ordinary and a little sad. The drift.
- **The Corrected Future:** Where you land if you take the signal read seriously, build the human-in-the-loop design in from the start, and make the decisions the drift future avoided.
- **The Preferred Future:** What you'd actually want this to produce — not what's realistic given current constraints, but what's worth designing toward.

**Facilitation note:** This tool works best when the room acknowledges there are genuinely multiple futures — not just the one they're hoping for. The word "drift" is doing real work. Not alarming. Just accurate.

**Artifact:** Divergent Futures Summary → Decision Register

### 4. Before You Build Analysis (Pre-Mortem)

**When to use:** At the start of any Build engagement. Before a major implementation decision. As a checkpoint before recommendations become commitments. For any team where the downstream human cost of getting it wrong is high.

**The method:** Before a project or implementation begins, imagine it has already failed. Specifically: it failed the people inside it. Work forward from that disaster to identify the decisions that led there — then design against them.

**Key questions:**
1. "Imagine it's two years from now. This implementation hurt the people it was supposed to help. What happened?"
2. "Who felt it first?"
3. "What decision, made before you started, led there?"
4. "What warning did you have that you didn't act on?"
5. "Now: how do you design against that?"

**The five disaster zones** (from HeyHi's "Don't Automate This" framework — use these to structure the pre-mortem):
- Design and creative judgment: what did the system start deciding that people used to decide?
- Team and organizational culture: what changed about how people related to each other?
- Client and stakeholder relationships: where did the system handle something that should have stayed human?
- Strategy and decision-making: what decisions did the system absorb that needed to stay owned by a person?
- Engineering and systems judgment: what broke that nobody could explain?

**Facilitation note:** Move quickly from disaster to design. The goal is not to scare — it's to surface what to build against. "We're not imagining this to scare ourselves. We're imagining it so we can design against it." Research shows pre-mortem analysis improves risk identification by approximately 30% over traditional risk analysis — because prospective hindsight activates different cognitive patterns than forward planning.

**Connection to HeyHi's work:** Before You Build is the gateway to any Build engagement. It is not a standalone workshop — it is the interrogation that determines whether the Build should happen, what it should look like, and who needs to be accountable for which outcomes. A client who runs Before You Build before a Build engagement makes a better client and gets better results. The five disaster zones map directly to the scope of HeyHi's Build work: design judgment, organizational culture, client relationships, strategic decision-making, and systems integrity. These aren't hypothetical failure modes — they're the categories where HeyHi has seen implementations go wrong.

**Artifact:** Decision Register

### 5. The Letter (Give Yourself an A)

**When to use:** As the anchor exercise in "The Preferred Future" workshop. As a closing exercise when the team has done hard diagnostic work and needs a path forward that feels real. In any session where abstract aspiration keeps crowding out specific direction.

**The method:** Write a letter dated three to five years from now, in past tense. The letter begins: "Dear [Name], We got there. Here's what happened." No "I hope," no "I plan," no conditional. It already happened. Aspiration invites doubt. Memory invites specificity — and specificity is where the strategic work lives.

**Instructions for participants:**
> Write a letter dated three to five years from now, written in past tense. Begin: "Dear [Name], We got there. Here's what happened." Past tense. Specific. Not what you hope — what happened.

**Key questions after writing:**
1. "What had to be true for this to happen?"
2. "What decision, made early, made the most difference?"
3. "Who was involved in making it possible?"
4. "What did you have to stop doing?"

**Facilitation note:** The letter can tip into wishful thinking if the room gets comfortable. Return often to "What had to be true for this to happen?" — that's what makes it strategic. Do not applaud individual responses. It turns reflection into performance. Observe the pattern, don't explain it: "What I'm noticing is most of what's up here is about judgment, craft, ownership — not a single person mentioned a tool." Let the room draw the conclusion.

**Connection to HeyHi's work:** The Letter consistently produces the same pattern across design teams: people write about ownership, craft, and judgment — not tools, not speed, not output volume. That pattern is itself the data. It tells a leadership team what their people actually value before any system is introduced or changed. For HeyHi, The Letter is diagnostic intelligence about organizational culture disguised as an aspiration exercise. Run it early in a Consult engagement to understand what the organization is trying to protect.

**Artifact:** Preferred Future Map (from The Letter sentences as raw material)

### 6. The Working Canvas (Futurecasting Canvas)

**When to use:** As the working artifact for any Consult engagement. As the deliverable from a full-day workshop. As the starting point for a Strategy Audit when a team wants to orient to the future, not just audit the present.

**The method:** A single-page synthesis of where an organization is, where it's headed, and where it wants to be. Built collaboratively during the session — not a post-session summary. The facilitator holds the frame; the room fills it in.

**Five sections (in this order):**

1. **Current Signals** — What are we noticing right now? What feels slightly off? (Named honestly. Not trends — precursors.)
2. **Current Trajectory** — If nothing changes, where does this organization land in three years? Be specific and honest.
3. **Preferred Future** — The specific, vivid future this organization is designing toward. Not abstract: "Our engineers can explain the systems they maintain. Our designers would put this work in their portfolios."
4. **The Gap** — What's between here and there? What decisions, structures, habits, or systems would have to change?
5. **Key Decisions** — Three to five decisions that will determine whether the preferred future is possible. Named, owned, dated.

**Optional sixth section:** "What's Being Avoided" — What decision does the team keep approaching and then stepping back from? Often the most valuable section.

**Lifecycle:** Produced in the room → reviewed and ratified by participants within 48 hours → lives as a living document, revisited quarterly.

**Connection to HeyHi's work:** The Working Canvas is the primary deliverable for Consult-tier engagements. It is what a client leaves the engagement with. Where a Design Intelligence Audit delivers an assessment, the Working Canvas delivers direction. The two artifacts are complementary: the Audit maps the current state of the design system, the Canvas maps the organizational trajectory around it. For multi-engagement clients, the Canvas from a previous session becomes the opening context for the next — it's the artifact that makes relationships compounding rather than episodic.

**Artifact:** Working Canvas + Decision Register

### 7. Field Reading (Horizon Scanning)

**When to use:** At the opening of a Strategy Audit. As part of Signal Readers workshop preparation. In thought leadership content as the "here's what's coming" foundation. When an organization only looks inward and needs peripheral vision.

**The method:** Systematically scan across domains (social, technological, economic, environmental, regulatory) for signals of change that might not yet be visible from inside the organization's immediate context. What's coming from outside that will matter inside.

**Key questions:**
1. "What's happening in adjacent fields — fields that affect yours — that you're not paying close attention to?"
2. "What regulatory or structural changes could shift how your team works in the next two years?"
3. "What are the organizations you most respect doing that you haven't started doing yet?"
4. "What's the thing that, five years from now, everyone will say was obvious? And are you paying attention to it now?"

**Domain scan framework (for the practitioner, not for the client):**
- **Technology:** What's happening in intelligent systems, automation, and infrastructure broadly that's heading toward this team?
- **Organizational design:** How is the structure of teams, the nature of roles, and the definition of expertise shifting in this industry?
- **Craft and quality:** What's happening to standards of quality in this field? What's being lost? What's being gained?
- **People and work:** How are people in this field talking about what they do and why? What are the recurring signals?

**Facilitation note:** This tool works best at the opening of a Strategy Audit or as preparation for Signal Readers. It builds peripheral vision — and organizations that only look inward are always surprised.

**Connection to HeyHi's work:** Field Reading is how HeyHi enters engagements with perspective the client doesn't yet have. Before a session, the practitioner scans the client's industry for external forces. This isn't research for its own sake — it's preparation for the moment in Signal Readers when the room says "we don't know where this is coming from." Field Reading lets the practitioner name what's coming before the client can name why they're unsettled. For thought leadership, Field Reading outputs feed directly into HeyHi's public writing and speaking — the "here's what's arriving in design and engineering teams" content that builds the firm's position.

**Artifact:** Signal Map (external scan section) → inputs to Working Canvas current signals

## The Diagnostic ↔ Generative Arc

These generative tools connect to the diagnostic work. The complete arc:

```
Diagnostic (past → present)        Generative (present → future)
─────────────────────────────────  ──────────────────────────────────
What went wrong?               →   What do we want instead?
What did it cost?              →   What decisions get us there?
What are we doing right now?   →   What are the early signals we're off-track?
Name the fracture.             →   Design the repair before it fractures.
```

A full engagement can run this arc end-to-end: open with Signal Readers (what are we noticing?), move through a diagnostic workshop (what's already wrong?), close with The Preferred Future or Before You Build (what are we designing toward?). Diagnosis without design is cynicism. Design without diagnosis is naivety.
