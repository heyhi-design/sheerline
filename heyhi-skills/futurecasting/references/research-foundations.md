# Research Foundations

The external research and methodology backing for HeyHi's strategic foresight practice. Use this reference for thought leadership, conference talk preparation, client education, or when a client asks about the research basis for the methodology.

This is a practitioner reference — not a bibliography. Each section gives you the core concept, the key sources, and how it connects to HeyHi's work.

---

## 1. Backcasting

**What it is:** Working backward from a desired future rather than forward from current conditions. Forecasting asks "what will happen?" Backcasting asks "what would have had to happen to get us here?"

**Research origin:** John Robinson (University of Waterloo, 1990) established the methodology foundations. K.H. Dreborg formalized the participatory approach in *Futures* (1996). Early adoption in Netherlands, Sweden, and Canada in energy policy and sustainability planning.

**Core insight:** Backcasting is especially useful when the current trajectory is clearly undesirable, when the problem connects to long-term decisions rather than short-term optimization, and when incrementalism won't close the gap. It was developed precisely for problems where forecasting (projecting current trends forward) was inadequate — because the whole point was to change the trajectory.

**HeyHi application:** The Preferred Future method is applied backcasting — start from the future the organization actually wants, work backward to the specific decisions that make it possible. The phrase "if this went right — really right — what would it look like?" is the backcasting prompt translated into HeyHi language.

**Limitation worth knowing:** Backcasting can produce aspirational futures disconnected from resource constraints if the facilitation doesn't push hard enough on what specifically has to change. The "what had to be true" question is the corrective.

## 2. Strategic Drift

**What it is:** The gradual deterioration that occurs when organizational strategies fail to keep pace with changes in the environment. Organizations experiencing strategic drift adhere to familiar, previously-successful strategies while the world around them transforms — producing slow-motion disconnection from reality.

**Research origin:** Gerry Johnson's work on strategic drift and the dominant paradigm. Karl Weick's research on organizational sensemaking (1988, 1995) explains the mechanism: organizations construct their environment through cycles of interpretation — and those cycles are biased toward continuity. The organizational paradigm (shared assumptions about what works) becomes the lens through which all new information is filtered. Signals that challenge the paradigm get reinterpreted to fit it.

**Core insight:** Strategic drift isn't negligence. It's the natural result of organizational culture doing its job — creating coherence and continuity. The problem is that the coherence it creates is backward-looking. "Escalation of commitment" (Staw, 1976) reinforces drift: the more an organization has invested in a particular direction, the harder it becomes to change course.

**HeyHi application:** The "current trajectory" section of the Working Canvas is a drift diagnosis. The question "if nothing changes, where does this organization land in three years?" asks the team to name their drift honestly. The Letter exercise and Divergent Futures mapping both work as drift interventions — they break the forward momentum of the current trajectory and substitute intentional direction.

**Facilitation note:** The drift future in scenario work should be named without catastrophizing. It's not alarming. It's just accurate. The phrase "quiet and ordinary and a little sad" is the right register. Drift doesn't announce itself — that's what makes it dangerous.

## 3. Weak Signals Theory

**What it is:** Early, subtle, often ambiguous indicators of emerging change that appear at the fringes of mainstream awareness before they develop into obvious trends. By the time signals are strong, options have narrowed.

**Research origin:** Igor Ansoff (1975) introduced the concept of weak signals as a strategic planning tool. His key insight: discontinuous change always has precursors that are visible to those who know how to look. The challenge isn't detection — it's interpretation. Most organizations have access to weak signals. Most organizations either don't see them or don't know what to do with them.

**Core insight:** Weak signals require cognitive diversity to interpret. What seems trivial to a manufacturing executive may be highly significant to a technologist. Organizations that can read signals across multiple domains and interpretation frames have a genuine early warning capability. Those that only look inward or only look at strong signals are structurally late to change.

**HeyHi application:** The Signal Readers workshop and the Organizational Early Warning tool are applied weak signal detection. The four signal categories (Meaning Erosion, Skill Atrophy, Trust Collapse, Accountability Void) are the organizational-specific filter — these are the categories HeyHi has found most reliably indicate emerging organizational crises. The "what feels slightly off right now?" question is the weak signal invite.

**Research finding worth citing:** Organizations with formal weak signal scanning processes are approximately 33% more likely to achieve above-average financial performance (Corporate Foresight Initiative research). The mechanism: early detection expands the response options available.

## 4. Scenario Planning and Divergent Futures

**What it is:** Developing multiple plausible future narratives based on different assumptions about how driving forces will play out. Not prediction — stress-testing decisions across multiple possible futures.

**Research origin:** Developed systematically at Shell in the 1970s (Pierre Wack). The strategic insight: organizations that plan for one future are fragile. Scenario planning builds robustness by forcing explicit consideration of multiple trajectories. Kees van der Heijden's *Scenarios: The Art of Strategic Conversation* (1996) formalized the facilitation methodology.

**The Futures Cone framework:** Organizes scenarios by epistemic category:
- **Possible:** Anything not physically impossible
- **Plausible:** Futures grounded in coherent causal logic from the present
- **Probable:** What current trends, if continued, produce
- **Preferred:** What the organization wants to be true

The Futures Cone's key insight is that the probable future (the one most organizations assume will occur) is only one of many plausible alternatives. Robust strategy should perform acceptably across multiple plausible futures, not just the expected one.

**HeyHi application:** Divergent Futures Mapping is the HeyHi translation of scenario planning. The three-scenario arc (Drift Future, Corrected Future, Preferred Future) is a simplified scenario structure optimized for organizational decision-making in workshop settings. The key question "which future are you currently building toward — intentionally or by default?" is the scenario planning question in HeyHi voice.

## 5. Pre-Mortem Analysis

**What it is:** Before a project begins, imagine it has already failed. Specifically: it failed the people inside it. Work backward from that failure to identify the decisions that led there — then design against them.

**Research origin:** Gary Klein (2007) developed the pre-mortem methodology based on "prospective hindsight." Research shows the technique reliably increases risk identification by approximately 30% over traditional risk analysis. The mechanism: imagining failure as already having occurred (rather than asking "what might go wrong?") activates different cognitive processes — it's easier to explain how something happened than to predict whether it will.

**Nobel-level endorsement:** Both Daniel Kahneman and Richard Thaler have endorsed the pre-mortem as a practical tool for improving decision quality. Kahneman writes about it in *Thinking, Fast and Slow*.

**Why it works better than traditional risk analysis:** Traditional risk assessment activates prevention-focused thinking — people identify risks defensively. Pre-mortem activates "what went wrong?" thinking — people generate explanations, which requires connecting causes to outcomes, which requires engaging with the specific organizational context more fully.

**HeyHi application:** Before You Build Analysis is applied pre-mortem. The five disaster zones (design judgment, team culture, client relationships, strategy, engineering) are the structural frame for the pre-mortem narrative — they ensure the disaster imagination covers the full scope of organizational impact, not just technical failure modes.

**Facilitation note:** Pre-mortems can spiral into fear if not facilitated carefully. The phrase "we're not imagining this to scare ourselves, we're imagining it so we can design against it" is the corrective. Move quickly from disaster identification to design response.

## 6. Horizon Scanning and Field Reading

**What it is:** Systematically scanning across multiple domains for signals of change that may not yet be visible from inside the organization's immediate context. Peripheral vision for organizations.

**Research origin:** The PESTLE/STEEP framework (Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal/Environmental) provides a scanning structure that prevents narrow-domain focus. The UK Government's Foresight Programme developed systematic horizon scanning protocols in the 1990s. Shell's scenario planning work was also foundational for field-scanning methodology.

**The Three Horizons framework** (McKinsey, 1999 — Baghai, Coley, White): Organizes external intelligence by time scale:
- **Horizon 1:** Near-term forces affecting current business (operating environment, competitive dynamics)
- **Horizon 2:** Medium-term emerging forces (developing technologies, regulatory shifts, demographic changes)
- **Horizon 3:** Long-term disruptive forces (fundamental shifts in how organizations operate, how work is organized, how value is created)

For futurecasting purposes, Horizon 2 and 3 scanning is most valuable — these are the forces that will shape the context the organization is navigating in 3–5 years. Horizon 1 scanning is already happening in most organizations' competitive intelligence functions.

**HeyHi application:** Field Reading is horizon scanning translated into HeyHi language. The "what's happening outside this room that's about to matter inside it?" question is the Horizon 2/3 scanning prompt. When activating the Predictive Layer for an engagement, the external scan uses STEEP-structured search to surface relevant external forces before the session begins.

## 7. Organizational Health as Leading Indicator

**What it is:** Organizational health metrics assess the cultural, structural, and relational dimensions of organizations that predict sustained performance. They are leading indicators — they reveal where an organization is heading before it shows up in financial metrics.

**Key frameworks:**
- **Gallup Q12:** 12 engagement items measuring role clarity, relationships, resources, recognition, and development. Decades of validation; highly consistent cross-culturally.
- **McKinsey Organizational Health Index (OHI):** 9 dimensions across 2,500+ organizations, 8M+ respondents. Validated 2023 research confirms top-quartile OHI organizations deliver 3× greater total shareholder returns over time.

**Why this matters for strategic foresight:** Organizational health is organizational change capacity. An organization with declining health scores is less capable of executing strategic shifts — regardless of how well-designed those shifts are. Scenario analysis without health assessment misses a critical variable: the organization's ability to actually get from here to there.

**Connection to the four signal categories:**
| HeyHi signal category | Q12 / OHI mapping |
|---|---|
| Meaning Erosion | Mission connection score, "my work matters" items |
| Skill Atrophy | Competence/capability items, development items |
| Trust Collapse | Manager trust score, psychological safety items, "someone cares about my development" |
| Accountability Void | Role clarity item (Q12 #1), clarity of expectations, decision ownership |

**Research on predictive analytics:** Current state of organizational analytics can flag high-turnover risk, forecast engagement decline, and surface skill gaps before they become critical. Key limitation: most organizations collect the data but lack the interpretation frameworks and organizational design practices to act on it. The Signal Map and Decision Register are HeyHi's structural response to this gap — connecting data patterns to specific named decisions.

## 8. The Letter Exercise: Academic Foundation

**What it is:** Writing a letter from a desired future, in past tense, describing what happened. The cognitive shift from "what I hope happens" to "what happened" produces markedly more specific and strategically useful output.

**Research origin:** Adapted from Benjamin Zander's "Give Yourself an A" exercise in *The Art of Possibility* (with Rosamund Stone Zander, 2000). Zander's original context: music education — students write a letter at the start of the year explaining why they received an A. The cognitive insight: framing the achievement as already accomplished activates different associations and removes the uncertainty that inhibits ambitious thinking.

**Why it works:** Aspiration invites doubt. Memory invites specificity. When you write from the future as though it already happened, you access a more concrete, sensory register than forward-looking planning. You say things you couldn't say as wishes — because you're explaining what happened, not predicting what will.

**Research backing for prospective hindsight:** Deborah Mitchell, J. Edward Russo, and Nancy Pennington (1989) demonstrated that "prospective hindsight" — imagining an event has already occurred — increases the ability to generate accurate reasons for why it happened. This is the same mechanism that makes pre-mortems effective for failure analysis. Both exercises use temporal reframing to access richer reasoning.

**Facilitation evidence:** In workshops where the Letter exercise is followed by backcasting questions ("what had to be true for this to happen?"), the exercise reliably produces specific, actionable direction that abstract goal-setting exercises don't. The most common pattern: almost nobody writes about tools or efficiency metrics. Almost everyone writes about judgment, ownership, relationship, or craft. That pattern is itself the data.

## 9. Causal Layered Analysis (for Depth Facilitation)

**What it is:** A futures methodology that analyzes situations across four layers: surface-level events (Litany), systemic drivers (Social/Structural), underlying worldviews (Discourse/Culture), and deep narrative patterns (Myth/Metaphor). Developed by Sohail Inayatullah.

**When to use in futurecasting:** CLA is most useful when conventional scenario planning produces scenarios that feel shallow — when the team has developed three scenarios but they all feel like variations of the same story. CLA surfaces the worldviews and cultural narratives that make each scenario coherent (or not), which deepens the organizational understanding of what it would actually take to inhabit a different future.

**HeyHi application:** CLA is an advanced facilitation tool — not a standard part of the seven-tool set, but available for complex engagements where the team needs to understand why certain futures feel possible and others feel foreign. The question "what would your organization have to believe — about itself, its people, its purpose — for this future to be real?" is a CLA-informed probe.

## For Thought Leadership and Conference Use

When preparing talks or articles that reference the research basis for the methodology:

- **Don't lead with the research.** The methodology earns trust through specificity, not citations. Research is in the background, not the foreground.
- **The Letter exercise**: credit to Benjamin Zander, *The Art of Possibility* (2000). "We've adapted an exercise from a music educator who started by giving every student an A."
- **Backcasting**: "Originally developed for energy policy research in the 1990s — we've applied it to organizational design."
- **Pre-mortem**: "Gary Klein's pre-mortem research shows this approach improves risk identification by about 30% — not because people work harder, but because the question activates different thinking."
- **Strategic drift**: "Gerry Johnson's research on strategic drift describes exactly what we're designing against: the slow-motion disconnection between where an organization thinks it's going and where it's actually headed."

The research backing is there to be cited when it strengthens the argument. It's never the argument itself.
